### printed 525 — ARTERIAL SUPPLY OF THE ORBITAL NERVE + Fig 15.48 → optic-nerve-blood-supply (was the single most-wanted missing page!)
- Pial plexus: anastomoses in a tortuous manner throughout the length of the orbital portion; receives branches along its length; at the junction of optic nerve and globe it is REINFORCED by branches from the scleral short posterior ciliary arteries or the arterial circle of Zinn.
- Multiple branches from the pial plexus enter the nerve substance CENTRIPETALLY to supply its axons, each lying within a central core of connective tissue lined externally with a glial coat of astrocytes.
- CRA origin variants (relative to ophthalmic artery course): (1) when the ophthalmic artery crosses ABOVE the nerve, the CRA arises independently or in conjunction with the medial posterior ciliary artery at the angle between the first and second parts of the ophthalmic artery; (2) when the ophthalmic artery crosses BELOW the nerve, the CRA arises independently as its SECOND branch.
- CRA course: runs forwards tortuously; pierces the dura 5-15.5 mm behind the globe INFEROMEDIALLY; its intravaginal course (0.9-2.5 mm) is mainly within the subarachnoid space; it enters the nerve VERTICALLY and makes a right-angled bend on reaching its central axis; branches: intraorbital ~5, intravaginal ~3, intraneural ~8, plus terminal retinal branches.
- Hayreh (1963) statistics: branches arise in orbit, sheath or nerve in ALL BUT 3.1% of nerves; NO branches were ever demonstrated to the lamina cribrosa (agreeing with Francois and Neetens, 1954); branches were lacking from the intraorbital part of the artery in 47.8% of nerves, from the intravaginal part in 5.3%, from the intraneural part in 25%.
- Collateral branches: lie mostly SUPERIOR when the ophthalmic artery is above the nerve and INFERIOR when it crosses below, with vessels arising less frequently on the opposite side of the nerve or medially; Hayreh (1963) rarely found collaterals on the LATERAL side although they were reported by Hughes (1954).
- Fig 15.48 (Hayreh, 1963): two patterns of ophthalmic artery branches when it crosses (1) under and (2) over the optic nerve.

### printed 526 — STRUCTURE OF THE CENTRAL RETINAL ARTERY + pial territory → optic-nerve-blood-supply + optic-nerve-sheath
- Intima: continuous endothelium on basement membrane, expanded by a zone of basement membrane-like material that THICKENS WITH AGE (Ikui, Tominaga and Mimura, 1964) and may contain smooth muscle cells and collagen in later decades, as in intracranial arteries (Flora, Dahl and Nelson, 1967; Anderson and Hoyt, 1969).
- Internal elastic lamina: similar to that of intracranial arteries, irregular particularly on the luminal side; DISAPPEARS at the lamina cribrosa (Anderson, 1969) and is ABSENT from the retinal arteries (Hogan and Feeney, 1963).
- GCA CORRELATE: in giant cell arteritis ONLY those vessels possessing an internal elastic lamina are affected — thus the posterior ciliary, choroidal and central retinal arteries are affected while the retinal arteries themselves show no direct involvement (Cogan, 1974).
- Media: about six layers of smooth muscle interspersed with collagen, elastin and basement membrane material (7-12 layers at the lamina in the monkey); no discrete external elastic lamina. Adventitia: dense connective tissue with occasional elastic fibres, CONTINUOUS WITH THAT OF THE OPTIC NERVE SEPTA.
- Pial territory of the CRA: intravaginal branches to the pia arise close to the entry of the artery, usually passing forwards and sometimes posteriorly in addition; the territory ALWAYS includes the INFERIOR surface of the nerve, the lateral and medial surfaces in about half the cases; the SUPERIOR surface is LEAST often supplied.
- Intraorbital branches of the CRA supply the pia from globe to canal in about half the nerves; pial branches anastomose with one another, with other pial vessels derived from ophthalmic branches and with recurrent pial branches from the choroid, scleral short ciliary arteries or circle of Zinn in the retrolaminar region; some anastomoses occur within the substance of the nerve.

### printed 530 — CIRCLE OF ZINN AND HALLER: frequency + replacement → optic-nerve-blood-supply + optic-disc
- FREQUENCY DISAGREEMENT: Olver, Spalton and McCartney (1990) report the circle is FREQUENTLY present, while Hayreh found it in only a SMALL PROPORTION of normal eyes; most authors agree it is often INCOMPLETE (Levitzky and Henkind, 1969) — possibly the source of the controversy.
- ABSENT in sub-human primates (Hayreh, 1964).
- In its complete form the 'circle' (usually a HORIZONTAL ELLIPSE) is an INTRASCLERAL anastomosis between branches of the MEDIAL and LATERAL paraoptic short posterior ciliary arteries (Olver, Spalton and McCartney, 1990).
- In the ABSENCE of the circular anastomosis its place is taken by small branches of the paraoptic short ciliary arteries, which lie within the sclera and supply portions of the optic nerve head and sometimes the adjacent retina.
- Must be distinguished from a more proximal EXTRASCLERAL anastomosis formed by separate short posterior ciliary arteries lying superiorly to the optic nerve in some eyes; incomplete upper/lower arcuate anastomoses also seen, sometimes overlapping non-anastomosing upper and lower segments; the circle may be anteriorly placed (closer to the choroid within the sclera) or more posteriorly placed (superior and inferior parts partially or completely extrascleral); according to its radius it may lie close to or further from the scleral opening.

### printed 535 — BLOOD-BRAIN BARRIER completion + OPTIC CHIASMA opening → optic-disc (HIGH-YIELD clinical)
- No barrier to diffusion across the highly fenestrated capillaries of the choroid (Cohen, 1973): potential route from the choriocapillaris along Bruch's membrane to the glial sheath of cells investing the axons in the prelaminar zone (border tissue of Jacoby).
- Tso, Shih and McLean (1975), horseradish peroxidase in monkeys: tracer entered the extracellular space of the choroid into that of the peripheral optic nerve at the prelaminar level; traced into the nerve head, across the glial sheath of astrocytes (prelaminar level), along the SEPTA of the lamina cribrosa into the RETROLAMINAR portion, and diffused around the axon bundles; confirms fluorescein studies of Grayson and Laties (1971) and human studies of McMahon et al. (1975) and Ben Sira and Riva (1975).
- GLIAL 'GASKET': tight junctions between the astrocytes making up the MARGINAL TISSUE OF KUHNT seal off the extracellular space of the optic nerve head from the peripapillary termination of the outer retina; diffusion across choriocapillaris and Bruch's membrane is impeded by the tight junctions between retinal pigment epithelial cells.
- CLINICAL: this arrangement "may have important implications for the behaviour of eyes with OPTIC PITS and PAPILLOEDEMA."
- 15.2 OPTIC CHIASMA: complete decussation in bony fishes/reptiles/amphibians/birds; mammals except monotremes have an uncrossed fraction that DECREASES as eyes become more forward-placed; equidae (panoramic) only 15% uncrossed, carnivora 25-30% (Walls, 1963); partial decussation first suspected by ISAAC NEWTON; confirmed anatomically by Gudden (1874-9) and Cajal (1909).
- Chiasma dimensions: ~13 mm transversely (range 10-20), 8 mm sagittal (range 4-13), 3-5 mm thick (Whitnall, 1932; Hoyt, 1969); lies obliquely continuing the 45-degree inclination of the optic nerves; overlies the sphenoid separated by a variable 0-10 mm (Traquair, 1916; Cope, 1916; Schweinitz, 1923).
- Fig 15.55 (Olver, Spalton and McCartney, 1990): optic nerve-chiasma-sellar dimensions (8 mm, 15 mm, 4 mm, 45°, 10 mm).

### printed 536 — chiasma location statistics + cistern + anterior relations → pathway context
- Location (Rhoton, Harris and Renn, 1977): 79% over the DORSUM SELLAE (most common); 12% PREFIXED over the diaphragma (tuberculum may project ~2 mm behind the anterior border of the chiasma); only 5% (also prefixed) in the SULCUS CHIASMATICUS; 4% POST-FIXED over and behind the dorsum (about 7 mm behind the tuberculum) (Schweinitz, 1923; Schaeffer, 1924; Bergland, Ray and Torack, 1968).
- Chiasma NOT in contact with the diaphragma — separated by 5-10 mm; part of the cisterna interpeduncularis lies inferior to it.
- Chiasmatic cistern: expanded subarachnoid region from the pituitary stalk forwards around the optic nerves into the olfactory sulci and above into the cisterna lamina terminalis; caudal part may be narrowed to a slit-like trabecular zone fusing with the arachnoid around the carotid arteries and over the inferior chiasma (Lindgren, 1957).
- ANTERIOR relations: anterior cerebral and anterior communicating arteries; the ACOM usually lies above the CHIASMA rather than the optic nerves (Rhoton, Harris and Renn, 1977); aneurysms of the ACOM or proximal A1 segment may compress the chiasma or one or both optic nerves.
- Fig 15.56 (Renn and Rhoton, 1975): prefixed/normal/post-fixed chiasma + 45-degree obliquity of the nerves.

### printed 537 — chiasma lateral/superior/inferior relations → pathway context
- LATERAL: continuous with the anterior perforated substance; the internal carotid artery ascending from the roof of the cavernous sinus is in CONTACT with the chiasma between optic nerve and tract; posteriorly the quadrilateral interpeduncular space; from the apex of the chiasma depends the infundibulum, joined to the posteroinferior chiasma at an ACUTE angle.
- SUPERIOR: floor of the third ventricle; continuous anteriorly with the lamina terminalis up to the anterior commissure — explains vulnerability to compression/stretching by third-ventricle tumours or raised ventricular pressure of internal hydrocephalus.
- INFERIOR: hypophysis; laterally the cavernous sinus with the oculomotor nerve closest on the diaphragma.

### printed 538 — NEURAL ORGANIZATION WITHIN THE CHIASMA → pathway context (topography continuity)
- Retinotopic distribution of retinal axons CONTINUES within the chiasma, but because of chiasmal decussation and the oblique placement, the detailed human organization is not precisely established; degree of retinotopy LESS than previously thought.
- Human data from clinical field-defect studies and autopsy of optic neuropathies (Henschen, 1893; Wilbrand and Saenger, 1906; Ronne, 1914); monkey degeneration support (Brouwer and Zeeman, 1925, 1926) culminating in the axon-tracing studies of HOYT AND LOUIS (1963) in HUMAN nerves.
- Fig 15.59: chiasmatic/interpeduncular/supracallosal cisterns.

### printed 540 — SEMIDECUSSATION DETAILS → optic-nerve (proximal nerve + Wilbrand knee)
- Uncrossed fibres travel backwards as a flattened compact bundle in the LATERAL part of the chiasma; in unilateral optic atrophy the uncrossed bundle of the fellow eye survives laterally as a RENIFORM area with its hilum medially — visible only in coronal sections.
- Crossed fibres (53% of total; Kupfer, Chumbley and de Downer, 1967) from the nasal hemiretinae spread as a roughly QUADRILATERAL sheet across almost the whole width of the optic nerve; inferior nasal fibres decussate ANTERIORLY, the most anterior looping forward into the proximal part of the CONTRALATERAL optic nerve for up to 3 mm — the interlacing basketwork = KNEE OF WILBRAND.
- CLINICAL: because of these anterior loop fibres, compression of the PROXIMAL optic nerve may affect the field of that eye AND that of the opposite side; these fibres probably cross LOW in the chiasma, so chiasmal involvement by pituitary tumours is often heralded by an UPPER bitemporal quadrantanopia (p. 541).

### printed 541 — macular fibres + commissures + chiasma vascular supply intro
- Superonasal fibres pass backwards somewhat laterally, mixing with uncrossed temporal fibres; the most lateral loop backwards into the ipsilateral tract before crossing to the superomedial contralateral tract (posterior loops less prominent than anterior).
- Actual decussation is CENTRAL in the chiasma; anterior fibres cross at more acute angles than posterior; fibres decussate side-to-side AND in ascending/descending directions (Traquair, 1957; Polyak, 1957; Duke-Elder and Cook, 1963).
- MACULAR FIBRES: central in the posterior optic nerve, maintain position entering the chiasma; crossing macular fibres pass obliquely upwards to decussate somewhat POSTERIORLY — Traquair's 'a little chiasma within the chiasma and within its posterior part'; macular axons occupy most of the CENTRAL chiasma to the exclusion of peripheral decussating fibres → lesions here cause a CENTRAL BITEMPORAL HEMIANOPIC SCOTOMA.
- Non-visual commissural fibres: dorsal commissure of GANSER and ventral commissures of GUDDEN and MEYNERT (Magoun and Ransom, 1942); existence doubted (Gitlin and Lowenthal, 1969).
- VASCULAR SUPPLY: rich collateral anastomosis of chiasmatic arteries — blockage of any single vessel unlikely to have a marked effect on visual fibres.

### printed 542-543 — CHIASMAL ARTERIAL SUPPLY (Wollschlaeger) → optic-nerve-blood-supply
- Fig 15.64 (Wollschlaeger et al., 1971): schematic of dorsal/ventral chiasmal and optic nerve arteries.
- Injection studies in 630 autopsy specimens (Wollschlaeger et al., 1971): (1) DORSAL supply mainly from the proximal (A1) segments of the anterior cerebral arteries + lesser supply from the internal carotid and anterior communicating arteries — Perlmutter and Rhoton (1976) ALWAYS found a contribution from central branches of the distal (A2) segment; (2) VENTRAL supply mainly from the internal carotid and anterior communicating arteries — unlike Bergland and Ray (1969), NO contributions identified from posterior communicating, posterior cerebral or basilar arteries.
- Small additional 'feeders' from the SUPERIOR HYPOPHYSEAL and MIDDLE CEREBRAL arteries.
- Classification: DORSAL chiasmatic arteries (antero- and posterodorsal branches) and VENTRAL (antero- and posteroventral branches); anastomoses between corresponding members of each group.
- Fig 15.65: two arteries supplying the right optic nerve.

### printed 544-545 — 15.3 OPTIC TRACTS opening → pathway context
- Optic tracts completely integral with the inferior cerebrum; fibres of nerve, chiasma and tract are axons of 'SECONDARY' neurons confined to the CNS.
- Tract relations: below and parallel the POSTERIOR CEREBRAL artery, even closer the ANTERIOR CHOROIDAL (arises from ICA lateral to the posterior communicating, lateral to the commencement of the tract); the AChA crosses below the tract to become medial at the anterior LGB; frequently a branch of the MCA too.
- The tract crosses the corticospinal tract in the middle segment of the cerebral peduncle, dorsal to the substantia nigra (lemnisci) — a single lesion may affect vision AND the large motor and sensory tracts; in the posterior internal capsule the radiations come close to all three pathways.
- Posteriorly the tract lies deep in the hippocampal sulcus (globus pallidus above, internal capsule medially, hippocampus below); approaches lateral (geniculate) and medial (collicular) 'roots'.

### printed 586-590 — VISUAL CORTEX organization (chapter 15 tail) → pathway context
- Fig 15.118: cytochrome oxidase 'blob' (dark) and 'interblob' regions in tangential layer 3; Fig 15.119 (Zeki): macaque V-area connectivity — Magno V1 (2→3, 4B, 4Ca) → V5/V3; Parvo V1 (2, 4A, 4Cb) → V4/V2; V2 → V3, V5, V4 (reciprocal with V1); V5 → V2 + layer IVb of V1; V5a → pons; V6 → tectum (Fries and Zeki, 1983).
- Corpus callosum: rostrum/genu/body/splenium; callosal fibres at the striate-prestriate boundary coincide with the VERTICAL MERIDIAN representation (Whitteridge, 1965) — uniting the two hemifields at the midline; V1-V1, V2-V2 connections with precision reflecting retinal mapping (narrow strip in V1; slightly broader V2/V3; diffuse V4/V5 — Zeki and Sandeman, 1976; Zeki, 1993); in cats callosal development is postnatal and mirrors binocular maturation; adult cat callosal axons confined to the striate/prestriate border (Innocenti et al., 1977; Schatz, 1977).
- 15.12 ORGANIZATION AND FUNCTIONS OF THE VISUAL CORTEX: centre-surround antagonism as the fundamental unit; geniculate neurons NOT involved in binocular integration — it begins in the columnar arrays of the striate cortex; colour perception depends on prestriate units.
- STRIATE CORTEX: precise columnar organization, functional units through the 2 mm cortical thickness (Figs 15.123, 15.124); IVC cells driven monocularly, 'circularly symmetrical' centre-surround receptive fields; hierarchical convergence: circular → SIMPLE (chiefly IVB, X/Y-like) → COMPLEX and HYPERCOMPLEX (layers I, III, V, VI outside IV; respond to line stimuli; complex less orientation-specific; hypercomplex restricted bar length + inhibitory outside field — Schiller, Finlay and Volman, 1976).
- OCULAR DOMINANCE COLUMNS: monocular columns alternate along a row; contralateral and ipsilateral eye columns ~500 um wide, several mm long, adjacent in regular array (Fig 15.124); magno LGN layers → IVC-alpha (lesser VI); parvo → IVC-beta (lesser IVA and VI); cortex projects back to LGN and claustrum (layer VI pyramids), to SC and pulvinar (layer V pyramids).
- Complex/hypercomplex cells receive from both eyes = BINOCULAR; 50% of cells in layers II, III, V and VI are binocular; binocular stimulation gives more vigorous response.
- Orientation columns: serial shift ~10 degrees every 20 um; a 180-degree cycle = 18 columns; hypercolumn (Hubel and Wiesel) = set of right+left dominance columns + full orientation cycle = functional unit of the retinotopic map.
- Blobs: cytochrome-oxidase-RICH regions contain wavelength-selective cells with peak responses at long 620 nm, middle 500 nm, short 480 nm (orange-red, green, blue); hypercolumns lie in CO-POOR 'interblob' regions; orientation-selective cells not wavelength selective (Dow, 1974; Gouras, 1974; Zeki, 1983); depth coding also present (Poggio et al., 1977).
- Fig 15.122: monocular deprivation — deprived-eye label occupies at most 38% of layer IVC (Swindale, Vital-Durand and Blakemore, 1981).
